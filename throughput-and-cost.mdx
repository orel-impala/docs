A GPU costs the same per hour whether it is saturated or idle. What changes is how many tokens come out of it.

That makes cost per token a throughput problem, not a procurement problem.

## Throughput is the lever

Production cost per million tokens is fully loaded hourly cost divided by tokens produced in that hour. The consequence is lopsided:

- **Double throughput** on the same hardware and cost per million tokens falls by roughly half.
- **Halve your electricity price** and it moves about five percent.

Power doesn't scale linearly with work either. A device draws somewhere between an idle floor and its thermal ceiling, and inference usually runs below that ceiling — so there is room for throughput to grow faster than watts, which means tokens per watt improve as the fleet gets busier.

This is why Impala runs hot rather than holding capacity in reserve. The full argument, with the arithmetic, is in [Run It Hot](https://www.getimpala.ai/blog/run-it-hot).

## What throughput costs

Keeping GPUs saturated means the scheduler is always packing work: batching requests together, interleaving prefill and decode, reordering against what the fleet is doing right now.

A request that arrives into a busy, well-packed fleet waits longer than one arriving into an idle machine held ready for it. That wait is the price of the utilization that produces the cost per token.

For [async work](/async-and-interactive), it's a price paid in a currency you don't spend — nobody is reading those tokens as they arrive.

## What you actually feel

Not per-request latency. **Task completion time** — wall-clock from submitting a task to getting the finished result.

For a batch run, that's dominated by throughput and by how much capacity sits behind your job. For an agent, it's per-step time multiplied by the number of steps, which is why turn count is usually a bigger lever than any individual call.

Two practical consequences:

- Benchmarking a single request tells you almost nothing about how the workload will perform. Measure a whole task, or a whole run.
- Client defaults are tuned for interactive providers. Raise your timeouts and turn off automatic retries — a retry on a queued request adds load without shortening the wait. See [Send a request](/send-a-request).

## Where the throughput comes from

**Continuous batching** — requests join and leave a running batch instead of waiting for a batch boundary, so the GPU rarely runs a partial workload.

**Prefix reuse** — agents re-read a large shared prefix on every step: system prompt, tool schemas, accumulated history. Caching that KV state and routing steps to workers that already hold it removes most of the recomputation.

**Dynamic scheduling** — routing and memory placement are decided continuously against live fleet signals rather than fixed at deploy time, so capacity follows the work.

**Tiered memory** — KV state moves between faster and slower tiers instead of pinning everything to the most expensive memory.

You don't configure any of this. It's the reason the cost per token looks the way it does.

## The trade, stated plainly

Impala gives up per-request latency guarantees and gets throughput and cost per token in return. For work with a person reading the output, that's a bad trade. For everything else, it's the whole point.
